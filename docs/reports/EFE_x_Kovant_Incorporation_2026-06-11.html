§2 The thesis — from audit trail to audit proof

An audit trail and an audit proof differ in one question: who has to be trusted for the record to be believed? A trail is declarative — it says what the platform chose to write, and whoever controls the storage can rewrite it without trace. For most software that is acceptable. For autonomous agents executing procurement and bidding for industrial customers, it is the soft spot every security review, every works council, every regulator will eventually probe: “your AI decided this — prove the record wasn’t edited after the fact.”

EFE’s answer is structural rather than procedural. Every agent call in a run becomes a canonical record — which agent, what it produced, when in the run’s logical order, from which inputs and predecessors — whose content is committed by a hash; the records are linked into an HMAC chain keyed per customer (tenant), whose final 32-byte value — the provenance_root — commits, transitively, the entire run. And one pure function, verify_swarm(records, key), replays the whole verification: every hash recomputed, every chain link checked, the causal graph validated (no invented ancestry, no phantom reads), the root rebuilt and compared. Mutate one output, delete one record, swap two steps — verification fails and names the exact record and reason. The function needs no network, no database, no EFE service, no Kovant service: records plus the tenant key, nothing else. Your customer’s auditor can run it in their own environment, years later.

Two further properties matter specifically to your positioning. Per-call forensics (the “N17” bridge): when agents run on SLMs, each agent record links — inside the sealed hash — to a forensic record of the underlying model call: model identity, hashes of system/user/final prompts, sampling parameters, token counts, retry count. “What every digital worker did and why” becomes answerable at the granularity of the individual model invocation, without storing prompt text in the clear (hashes prove what was sent without exposing it). Honest scope: EFE never promises bit-for-bit reproducibility of an LLM — nobody can. It promises forensic re-proof (integrity + lineage + exact parameters), and offers a fully deterministic agent mode whose outputs are reproducible, as the anchor for demos and CI.

The one-sentence pitch to your enterprise customers. “Every action your digital workforce takes is sealed into a cryptographic chain at execution time; here is the verifier — run it yourself, offline, against your own copy of the records. You do not have to trust Kovant, and Kovant does not have to trust its model providers.” That sentence converts your System of Work and Record from a feature into a differentiator no copilot vendor can match by adding logging — because the value is not in recording, it is in re-provability without trust, and that is a property of the data format, not of the service.

3.1 — Blueprint step → swarm spec: the load-bearing mapping

A Blueprint, in your words, captures “the mission, rules, handoffs, and job descriptions for each digital colleague.” EFE’s request object captures the same four things in executable form: the mission is the task payload; the job descriptions are the agent names in the spec (in SLM mode, the name is the instruction the model receives — “Classify the topic in one word…” is a job description in the most literal sense); the handoffs are the topology (a Pipeline’s stage order, a Hierarchical’s decompose→solve→merge, a Consensus’ generate→judge→aggregate); and the rules are the budget cap with its policy (stop / error / degrade-to-fit) plus the engine guardrails. The consequence for your product: a Blueprint step doesn’t need a new authoring model — it needs a compiler, and the target language is a one-page JSON spec.

3.2 — Why the team shapes matter to your SLM conviction

Your launch materials argue SLM swarms reduce error versus one large generalist. EFE’s gates quantified when that is true and what coordination it takes — and the numbers are directly reusable in your sales conversations. On verifiable tasks, the right answer is usually present among K cheap candidates (90.5% at K=7, measured on 2,499 prompts × 11 real models) but minority — majority voting collects only 39.6%. A deterministic a-posteriori verifier (T1) collects 90.0% — beating a monolith (80.1%) at 2.9× lower cost. Where no single verifier exists, a five-judge ordinal consensus (Borda) reaches 0.716 with judges of merely 0.70 skill — and a real 12-B SLM judge measured at skill 1.00 on the gate protocol. This is your “reduced hallucination risk” claim, turned into artefacts: not SLMs are good enough, but SLMs + the right selection topology are measurably better and cheaper, here is the JSON.

3.3 — Mission Control plugs into seams, not into agents

Everything Mission Control needs is emitted by the engine’s observer surface: run started (topology, mode, cap), each agent call (output, cost, live budget snapshot), seal (records count, traced %, root, verified), finish. Two properties matter for your integration cost: the observer is attached with one builder line and zero changes to agents or Blueprints ; and observability is constitutionally lossy while the proof chain is not — a flooded dashboard can drop frames, the sealed record never does. Your exception-management UX stays exactly where it is; it just gains a feed that can never disagree with the audit, because the audit is computed from the same sealed records.

3.4 — The boundary: what EFE refuses to own

The Integration Hub — credentials into SAP, Salesforce, Teams, the 25+ systems — remains entirely yours, and this is by design, not omission: EFE’s SDK has a CI-enforced guarantee of zero network and TLS dependencies (a test reads the source and fails if any appear), which is precisely what makes its security review short. EFE executes and proves; it does not connect. Likewise the Concierge, the HITL approval UX, the Blueprint designer: yours. The engine’s ambition stops at a boundary your architecture already drew — which is why the two stacks compose instead of competing.

§4 Integration architecture

Where does EFE physically sit in a Kovant deployment? One sentence: a stateless proof-and- execution service behind your Agent Runtime, one instance per customer tenant, inside the customer’s own cloud tenant — exactly where you already deploy. The rest of this section unpacks that sentence.

4.1 — The placement, and the request flow

Your Agent Runtime keeps its role as the conductor: it receives work from Blueprints, talks to the Integration Hub for enterprise data, and manages human-in-the-loop. The change is what happens when a Blueprint step says “now a team of digital colleagues produces and validates a result”: instead of hand-rolling that loop per framework, the Runtime POSTs one JSON document to the EFE gateway — { task, topology, budget, mode } — and receives the complete outcome: the output, the accounting, the live-verified provenance summary (root + verified: true), the per-call forensics summary, and a run_id. The Runtime stores the run_id in your System of Work and Record next to its own trail entry; from that moment, GET /verify/:id — or the offline verifier — can re-prove that step forever. Exceptions flow the other way: a low-confidence outcome (the engine reports its own hesitation) becomes a Mission Control exception, feeding your existing HITL loop.

4.3 — Tenancy: your customer = our tenant, cryptographically

EFE’s isolation unit aligns one-to-one with yours. Each Kovant customer becomes an EFE tenant, which buys three properties at once. Key isolation: the proof chain is keyed per tenant via HKDF-SHA256 — customer A’s verification key proves nothing about customer B’s runs; you can hand each customer their key (or escrow it) without cross-exposure. Storage isolation: every persisted run is tenant-scoped at the query level — a run fetched with the wrong tenant simply does not exist (this is tested, including against a second tenant probing a first tenant’s run_id). Policy isolation: PII redaction policy (Off / Detect / Redact / Strict) is set per tenant — your GDPR story per customer, not per platform. Authentication closes the loop: the gateway verifies OIDC JWTs (ES256, public key only — it can validate tokens minted by your existing IdP, Entra ID included) and maps the token’s tenant claim to all of the above. Your Runtime authenticates once; isolation follows the token.

4.4 — The keys story: what your ops team holds, and what it never touches

This is the part your security reviewers will like. A full Kovant×EFE deployment involves exactly one secret on the EFE side: the HKDF master (an environment variable, mounted from your KMS / Kubernetes Secret), whose only power is to sign provenance — it decrypts nothing, grants access to nothing. The engine’s client and SDK are keyless by construction (no field exists where a key could go; a CI test scans the source for auth headers and network deps). Frontier-model access — when a Blueprint step genuinely needs one — goes through EFE’s Network mode: the gateway translates the request and forwards to an OpenAI-compatible router that you operate (a LiteLLM-class proxy, or your existing model gateway), which holds the provider keys in its environment. EFE never sees them; its forensics still capture which model served each call, and budget governance still bills the step at the Network tariff (this exact property — the tariff surviving the translation — was found by an end-to-end test and is now regression-locked). PII adds one more sovereign touch: detection is local regex — no sidecar call, compatible with egress-deny — and redaction applies to presentation surfaces only, never to the sealed records, so redaction policy can evolve without invalidating a single proof.

4.5 — Deployment in your customers’ tenants

The shipped Helm chart encodes the sovereign default that matches your Nordic industrial customers’ expectations: the gateway pod with an Ollama sidecar (the SLM runs on localhost inside the pod), a NetworkPolicy of egress-deny with a single named exception (the router namespace, if Network mode is enabled at all), the HKDF master from a Secret, liveness/readiness on /metrics, graceful SIGTERM for clean rollouts, non-root runtime. Postgres (your customers’ managed instance) carries the sealed runs as JSONB, write-once. The air-gapped variant is the same chart minus the router exception — fully offline operation, which is not a degraded mode but the flagship one: the entire P5 gate (three real SLMs, every criterion at 1.0) ran in exactly that configuration.

§6 The incorporation plan — gates, not dates

We propose the adoption follow the same discipline that built the engine: four gates, each with a pre-agreed measurable criterion, each cheap to abort. No phase starts before the previous verdict; every verdict leaves an artefact. Calendar effort is small — the work at each gate is hours to days — but the decision rhythm belongs to the verdicts, not the calendar.

Gate 0 — Keyless proof-of-fit (one engineer, one day)

Do: run the gateway standalone; compile one real Operations Blueprint step (we suggest a classification or extraction step from your procurement flow) into a swarm spec; execute it keyless (deterministic agents) through POST /v1/swarm/run, then hit GET /verify/:id. Criterion: the step round-trips — output usable, verified: true, root displayed — and your team agrees the spec JSON is a sane compile target for Blueprints. What it derisks: the conceptual mapping of §3, at zero infrastructure cost (no model, no database, no keys — laptop-grade).

Gate 1 — SLM pilot on your tasks (≈ one week)

Do: point the same gateway at an Ollama serving your preferred SLM, in self-host mode; re-run the P5 campaign protocol on a sample of your Blueprint tasks (the harness ships in the repo); wire the SSE event stream into a Mission Control panel prototype. Criterion: P5’s own bar — verify = traced = 1.0 on every run, budget within cap, tamper-injection detected — plus a subjective one: your operators see the swarm live in Mission Control. What it derisks: quality and latency on your real workload, with your models, under the trust criteria your customers will inherit.

Gate 2 — Production slice in one customer tenant (≈ two–three weeks, mostly yours)

Do: deploy the Helm chart into one (friendly) customer’s cloud tenant — Ollama sidecar, egress-deny, HKDF master from their KMS, Postgres persistence, OIDC against your IdP; route one production Blueprint through it; hand the customer’s audit/compliance contact the records export and the offline verifier. Criterion — the one that matters: the customer’s auditor re-verifies a production run offline, on their machine, and the root matches. The day that happens, “audit proof” stops being a slide. What it derisks: operations (rollouts, sizing, observability) and the compliance story, end-to-end.

Gate 3 — Fleet & frontier (scale-out)

Do: extend to more Blueprints and tenants; enable Network mode through your existing model gateway for the steps that justify frontier quality, keys staying in your router; turn on per-tenant PII policies per customer contract. Criterion: the in-process Network test’s assertions, observed in your environment — mapped model correct, spent_cents non-zero and ≤ cap, N17 bridge fully consistent — plus the admission-control mitigation of §7 in place before broad multi-tenant exposure.

Why this plan is cheap to say yes to. Gate 0 costs a day and produces, even on failure, a precise incompatibility list. Each later gate reuses the artefacts of the engine’s own validation (the P5 harness, the Network test, the Helm chart) rather than inventing bespoke pilot machinery. And the plan’s shape is itself the compatibility test: if gate-driven adoption feels natural to your team, the two engineering cultures fit — which, for an engine you are putting inside your product, matters as much as the benchmarks.

One engine, one moat, a clean seam to everything you have already built.

The ask. One engineer, one day, Gate 0 — a laptop, no keys, no infrastructure. The output is either a working verifiable Blueprint step and a JSON spec your team has opinions about, or a precise list of why not. Both outcomes are worth the day.
